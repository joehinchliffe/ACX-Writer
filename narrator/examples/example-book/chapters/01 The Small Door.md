# Chapter 1: The Small Door

Most people imagine change as a wall to climb. They picture the whole height of it at once, and the picture alone is enough to send them back to the sofa. But the people I have watched change their lives rarely climbed anything. They found a small door, opened it, and walked through before they had time to argue with themselves.

Consider Maria. At 43 she had tried to start running six times. Each attempt began with a plan: five mornings a week, 10 kilometres by spring, a new pair of shoes that cost more than she wanted to admit. Each attempt ended in the second week, usually on a wet Tuesday, with the shoes back in the cupboard and a quiet feeling of failure that lasted much longer than the running had.

"I don't think I'm a runner," she told me. "I think I'm someone who buys running shoes."

We laughed, and then we tried something else. Her only task for the first week was to put the shoes on at 7:15 every morning and step outside the front door. That was it. If she wanted to come back in, she could. If she wanted to walk to the end of the street, fine. The rule was simply that the shoes went on and the door opened.

## Why the door works

The first reason is obvious once you say it out loud: you cannot fail at something that small. A plan that asks for 40 minutes of effort on a cold morning gives your tired mind a hundred reasons to say no. A plan that asks for 30 seconds gives it almost none. Dr. Hartley, who studied habit formation for more than 20 years, put it this way: "The size of the first step should be chosen by the person you are on your worst day, not your best."

The second reason is less obvious. Every time you walk through the small door, you cast a vote for a new identity. Maria was not yet running, but she was becoming someone who goes outside in her running shoes every morning. After nine days she noticed that she no longer argued with herself about it. After three weeks she was jogging to the end of the road and back, not because the plan said so, but because she was already outside and it felt silly not to.

The third reason is momentum. Starting is the most expensive part of almost any task. Once the shoes are on, the hard part is over, and the rest of the morning tends to follow the first decision.

## Finding your own door

So how do you find the small door in your own life? Start by writing down the change you want, in one plain sentence. Then ask yourself a single question: what is the smallest visible action that a person who had already made this change would do every day?

For a writer, it might be opening the document and typing one sentence. For someone who wants to save money, it might be moving $5 into a savings account every Friday. For someone who wants to sleep better, it might be putting the phone on the kitchen table at 10 p.m. and leaving it there.

Write your answer down. Then make it smaller. Then make it smaller again, until the idea of skipping it feels almost ridiculous.

Will this feel too easy? Yes, at first. That is the point. You are not trying to prove anything to anyone in the first week. You are building a doorway, and doorways are supposed to be easy to walk through.

## What happens next

In the next chapter we will look at what to do when the small door stops being enough, and how to widen it without losing the ease that made it work in the first place. For now, choose your door, and open it tomorrow morning.

Maria, by the way, ran her first 5 kilometre race fourteen months later. She still keeps the original shoes by the front door. "They're not for running any more," she said. "They're there to remind me how small the beginning was."
